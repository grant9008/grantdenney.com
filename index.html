Live · Java · Official RuneLite Plugin Hub

Personal Space

Old School RuneScape only draws one player per tile. Stand six people on the same square at a bank and five of them disappear. Personal Space draws the ones the game hides, spread out around their tile, so a packed room actually looks packed.

  • ~171k views, 1.4k upvotes and 238 comments in its first two hours when I posted it to r/2007scape — overwhelmingly positive, and people were installing it that day
  • Cosmetic only, on purpose. Jagex forbids moving the click zones of 3D components, so nothing real moves: not positions, not click targets, not overheads. Only what gets drawn
  • Working out why the obvious fix failed meant decompiling the client. Actors draw sorted but without depth, and the GPU plugin batches a whole frame of them into one pass with depth writes off — so whoever is drawn later ends up on top, however far away they are. Moving a model toward the camera does nothing. The fix was re-sorting the draw pass itself
  • Crowds that behave. Four formations, plus rules for bank counters, anvils, fires and riverbanks so people line up facing the thing they’re using instead of standing inside each other
  • At 1.8.55 after repeated Hub reviews. Every release needs a fresh pull request into runelite/plugin-hub and another maintainer pass — a merged one can’t be reused

Prototype · TypeScript + PixiJS · AI-assisted

RealmForge

A dark-fantasy mobile RPG: make a character, walk a hand-laid town, build your own plot piece by piece, send expeditions out for loot. It runs in a browser, installs to a phone home screen, and builds an Android APK on every push.

  • 41,488 lines of TypeScript across 98 files, in five days. 95 commits between 27 September and 1 October 2026
  • The renderer is hand-written. Isometric projection, A* pathfinding, depth sorting, an additive light layer with flicker, drifting fog — on PixiJS, with no game engine and no physics library
  • A builder with 39 placeable piece types, five plot sizes, multi-storey construction, terrain height editing and autosave; plus a town of 15 buildings and 202 props with named residents
  • Procedural audio with no audio files — a generative lute songbook that re-ornaments and humanises its timing on every performance
  • It checks its own work. A layout validator fails the build on props over cliffs, unreachable doors or sealed pockets; a balance harness re-runs the real dice resolver under exact DP and Monte Carlo against the design document’s own target tables
  • What’s actually mine: the design document, every call about what the game should be, and the judgement about what was worth building. The art is AI-generated and the code came out of AI pair-programming. That’s the point — this is what the rest of this page is describing, done at speed

The part nobody sees

A scooter is a bad shape to wrap. Compound curves, cutouts, hardware in the way, and a deck that has to survive being stood on. The product isn’t the vinyl — it’s the template underneath it.

This is a real production cut file: one G30P, sixty-four panels, each one shaped to the piece of the scooter it covers. 3D scanning gets the geometry, digital cutting software turns it into panels, and the result is a kit that lands on the part without trimming, stretching or guesswork.

It’s also why brand work is possible at all. Hand a designer a template that already fits and they can put any artwork on top of it — which is exactly what Segway and Red Bull did with the go-karts.

Dale Carnegie, Los Angeles

Fifty-two hours across two courses in 2019 — Skills for Success and Sales Advantage — taken in person in 2019, while I was out in LA.

Both were run by the same coach, Brian Barry — a Dale Carnegie trainer who also works as a magician. That turns out to be exactly the right background for the subject. Most of what stuck came from watching him do it rather than from the workbook.

The practical version: open with the other person’s problem, not your product. Handle the objection they haven’t said out loud yet. And be genuinely easy to deal with, which is not a technique.

A bit about me

Figuring it out myself

Take the thing apart, work out what’s actually wrong, and don’t pay someone else to guess. That’s the habit behind everything on this page — including the part where I finally got past what stopped me the first time.

Why I build

I always wanted to code. I went to UNO assuming that liking computers and games would make me a programmer. It didn’t. Java and I never meshed, and I failed it. For a long time that was the end of the story.

What changed isn’t that I became a different person — the bottleneck moved. AI tooling took technical know-how off the critical path, and what’s left is the part I was always any good at: deciding what to build, how it should work, and where it goes next. I’m the one making the calls on design and direction. I’m picking up the code bit by bit as I go, on projects people actually use, instead of on a course I failed.

Both products started the same way — I didn’t want to pay for the tool. PocketGE and the Flip Tracker exist because I wasn’t paying for Flipping Copilot. iRaceHUD exists because I wasn’t buying yet another sim-racing overlay just to see my delta mid-lap. Same instinct as the cars: I’d rather work it out than pay someone to.
